Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are being adopted in financial work-
flows, where accuracy and reliability are critical. One such applica-
tion is financial question answering over public company filings.
However, LLMs struggle with quantitative financial reasoning, in-
cluding identifying financial facts, interpreting reporting periods
and units, and determining appropriate metrics, leading to incor-
rect answers. Recent RAG-based methods have improved financial
question answering but do not ensure factual and numerical cor-
rectness. LLM-based verifiers may also accept incorrect numerical
claims. To address this, we introduce VeriFin, a neurosymbolic
framework for verifying LLM generated numerical financial claims.
Given a question and generated answer, VeriFin retrieves filing
evidence, grounds values in XBRL facts, and establishes the re-
quired formula independently of the answer. It then uses the Z3
SMT solver to check the resulting arithmetic constraints. VeriFin
accepts consistent claims, rejects inconsistent claims, and abstains
when required facts or formulas cannot be established. To evaluate
VeriFin, we constructed XBRLFiling, a dataset of 600 numerical
questions from corporate filings, and selected 67 and 403 questions
from FinanceBench and TAT-DQA, respectively. Across six an-
swer models and three datasets, VeriFin achieves 100% observed
precision among accepted answers with known correctness. For
Qwen3-30B on XBRLFiling, VeriFin accepts 304 correct answers
versus 166 for an LLM judge, while reducing false accepts from 87
to zero. We release our benchmark to support research on reliable
financial question answering.

However, a fluent answer may rely on the wrong year, scale, sign,
metric definition, or source fact while remaining entirely plausible.
Once incorporated into a spreadsheet, valuation model, risk report,
compliance assessment, or investment memo, such an error may
be difficult to detect and trace. The central reliability problem is
therefore not only whether an LLM can generate a financial claim,
but whether that claim should be accepted into a financial workflow
or used in financial decision-making. For practical use, a plausible
numerical answer is not enough; it must be independently verified
before acceptance.
Recent works have improved financial question answering through
retrieval, structured XBRL access, calculation tools, and executable
programs [7, 8, 10, 16], nonetheless, numerical reliability remains a
challenge. FinanceBench, XBRL-Agent, and Fin-RATE show that
errors continue even when models have access to filings and ex-
ternal tools, particularly when questions require the correct entity,
reporting period, line item, or computation [7–9]. The problem
is therefore not only retrieving the relevant evidence or execut-
ing arithmetic, but establishing whether the selected facts, units,
periods, and formula actually supports a candidate answer. Com-
plementary work on atomic claim grounding, financial-statement
consistency, and XBRL-based audit verification addresses parts of
this problem [6, 15, 17]; however, a model-generated program may
execute the wrong computation, retrieved evidence may be insuf-
ficient, and an LLM judge may still approve a claim that does not
follow from the filing. We call the acceptance of such an incorrect
numerical claim a false accept. This motivates the operational ques-
tion at the center of our work: given a fixed numerical claim
produced by an LLM, should a financial workflow accept it?
To address this question, we introduce VeriFin, a filing-grounded
neurosymbolic framework that separates claim generation from
verification and determines whether numerical claims produced by
LLMs should be accepted (Figure 1). VeriFin treats each candidate
claim as an untrusted claim. First, it grounds the required financial
values in filed XBRL facts, preserving their concepts, reporting
periods, units, and contexts. Second, it establishes the required
calculation independently of the candidate claim. The calculation
is obtained from an operation stated in the question, the filing’s
XBRL calculation relationships, a documented financial metric defi-
nition, or a filing-specific definition. Third, VeriFin compiles the
grounded values, calculation, and candidate claim into arithmetic
constraints checked by the Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT)
solver Z3 [5]. The framework returns Verified when the candidate

We evaluate VeriFin on XBRLFiling, a new benchmark of 600
numerical questions constructed from calculations in 10-K filings of
28 U.S. companies, and on a numerical subset of FinanceBench. On
XBRLFiling, VeriFin records zero observed false accepts, whereas
the four baselines accept between 6 and 92 of the same 92 incorrect
claims. VeriFin achieves 92.2% accuracy among decided cases with
1.2% abstention. On FinanceBench, VeriFin again records zero
observed false accepts, whereas the baselines accept between 4 and
21 of the same 21 incorrect claims. The zero false-accept result
persists across multiple answer-generator models. These findings
shift the reliability question in LLM-based financial analysis from
whether a claim sounds plausible to whether filed facts and an
independently established calculation support its acceptance. We
evaluate VeriFin across six answer models on XBRLFiling, our
new benchmark of 600 questions, and selected numerical subsets of
FinanceBench [8] and TAT-DQA [21]. Across all 18 experimental
settings, we find 100% precision among accepted answers with
known correctness. Our strongest comparison shows improvements
in both precision and the acceptance of correct answers: for Qwen3-
30B on XBRLFiling, VeriFin accepts 304 correct answers compared
with 166 for LLM Judge, while reducing false accepts from 87 to zero.
This represents 83% more correct answers accepted from the same
candidate pool. These results demonstrate that effective verification
can improve the reliability of accepted answers while allowing more
correct answers to pass.
Our contributions are:
• We formulate verification of numerical financial claims as an
acceptance-control problem, focused on the risk of accepting
incorrect claims and the need to abstain when evidence is
insufficient. The objective is to reduce the risk of passing
unsupported claims into downstream financial workflows.
• We develop VeriFin, a framework that grounds required
values in filed XBRL facts, establishes the financial calcula-
tion independently of the candidate claim, and checks the
resulting claim using a symbolic solver.
• We introduce XBRLFiling, a 600-question benchmark con-
structed from corporate filings, and evaluate VeriFin on
XBRLFiling, FinanceBench, and TAT-DQA.
Neurosymbolic Verification. Logic-LM, LINC, and SatLM trans-
late natural-language problems into formal representations and
delegate inference to symbolic solvers [13, 14, 18]. Similarly, ARc
uses inference-time autoformalization to verify natural-language
policy rules with automated reasoning tools [2]. Although inference
is deterministic with respect to the generated formal representa-
tion, semantic-parsing errors can still lead to incorrect conclusions.
VeriFin reduces reliance on unconstrained free-form translation
by grounding numerical values in XBRL facts and restricting com-
putations to operations specified in the question, filing calculation
relationships, or documented financial definitions. Given a com-
plete and correctly grounded verification plan, VeriFin uses Z3 [5]
to deterministically check whether the candidate claim satisfies the
resulting arithmetic constraints.

Related Work
Factuality and LLM-based evaluation. Benchmarks such as Truth-
fulQA and FActScore demonstrate that fluent language-model out-
puts are not necessarily factually supported [11, 12]. LLM-as-a-
judge methods use one language model to evaluate the output of
another, providing a flexible alternative to task-specific evaluators.
However, their decisions can exhibit systematic biases and reason-
ing failures [20]. In numerical financial settings, these limitations
are particularly consequential because a plausible explanation may
conceal an incorrect operand, reporting period, unit, or calculation.
Financial claim verification. FinDVer[19] studies explainable claim
verification over long, hybrid-content financial documents under
long-context and retrieval-augmented settings. Its RAG-CoT ap-
proach conditions an LLM on retrieved evidence to produce an
explained binary verdict [19]. VerifierFC[4] addresses reasoning
drift in complex numerical claim verification through test-time
scaling, generating multiple reasoning paths and using a trained
path ranker to select the highest-scoring path and associated ver-
dict [4]. These approaches rely on model-generated judgments or
reasoning paths, whereas VeriFin checks a fixed candidate claim
against independently established financial evidence and arithmetic
constraints.
Executable and guided numerical reasoning. Program-of-Thoughts
delegates arithmetic reasoning to executable model-generated pro-
grams [3], and SEC-QA applies this paradigm to numerical ques-
tions over SEC filings [10]. These approaches separate numerical
execution from natural-language generation, but the generated
program still specifies the operands and computation to execute.
VERAFI incorporates formalized financial policies as guidance dur-
ing agentic generation [1]. These methods are complementary to
post-generation verification, where the objective is to determine
whether an already generated numerical claim is sufficiently sup-
ported for acceptance.

3
Methodology
VeriFin separates answer generation from verification. Given a fi-
nancial question, a candidate numerical answer, and filing evidence,
it checks whether the answer follows from supported financial facts
and a documented calculation. The framework accepts consistent
claims, rejects inconsistent claims, and abstains when the required
support cannot be established.
3.1
Neurosymbolic Verification
Financial verification requires identifying the appropriate concepts,
reporting periods, units, and calculation from the filings, then check-
ing whether they support the generated claim. As illustrated in
Figure 1, we use language models to semantically interpret the
question and propose verification plans. Then, these proposals are
checked against filing evidence, XBRL calculation relationships and
documented metric definitions to establish the financial inputs and
calculations independently of the candidate claim.
The supported facts, calculation, and candidate value are encoded
as constraints and checked for consistency using the SMT solver Z3.
The decision of the solver is conditional on the facts and calculation
encoded in those constraints. We next describe the five components
of the framework.

3.5
Provenance Verifier
Grounding Operands in XBRL Facts. For each required operand
role 𝑟𝑖, the Provenance Verifier independently establishes four prop-
erties: the referenced taxonomy concept, the reporting period, the
reported unit, and the filed value.
Concept. The role is resolved to a taxonomy concept in three
stages. If the filing’s calculation linkbase identifies a child concept
for the role under the requested subtotal, that concept is bound
directly. Otherwise the role is matched against a curated concept
registry that maps normalized financial concepts to candidate tax-
onomy elements (e.g., us-gaap:AccountsPayableCurrent). If nei-
ther stage resolves the role, a symbolic label-matching heuristic
tokenizes the filing row label and candidate concept labels, accepts
a concept only when the row’s tokens are a subset of the concept
label, and breaks ties by selecting the candidate with the fewest
surplus tokens.
Period and unit. A concept alone does not uniquely identify a fact:
the same concept is reported for multiple fiscal periods and may
be presented at different display scales (e.g., thousands or millions
of USD). A role therefore binds only to a fact whose reporting
period matches the question and whose unit matches the expected
measure, after normalizing the reported value to the requested scale.
A concept that exists in the filing but has no fact at the required
period and unit does not bind.
Value and provenance. The operand value is taken exclusively
from the filed fact. Registry-resolved concepts are therefore bound
value-blind: the filed value overrides whatever figure the model
extracted. Consequently, structural mismatches (e.g., binding Pr
ofitLoss where the authorized policy requires NetIncomeLoss)
are not filtered during grounding but instead surface later as solver
constraint violations. Only the heuristic third stage is guarded
by a plausibility check against the extracted value. Every binding
records the concept, reporting period, unit, accession number, and
source fact identifier, so each admitted operand is fully traceable
to a specific reported filing fact. If any required role cannot be
grounded, no verification plan is admitted and VeriFin abstains.

constraint system yields Violated; the system returns Abstain
when a required operand or authorized formula cannot be estab-
lished or when the solver returns unknown. Constraints are named
so that a violation can be accompanied by an unsatisfiable core
identifying conflicting filing, formula, or claim constraints. The
final solver decision is deterministic conditional on the grounded
facts and authorized computation.

fication can be traced to the relevant filing value, formula condition,
or claim bound. For a verified claim, the Z3 SMT Solver returns
logically grounded evidence supporting the result. For a Violated
claim, it identifies a conflicting subset of named filing, formula,
and claim constraints that cannot all hold together, thereby pin-
pointing the source of the error. The violated claim bound further
indicates whether the claim overstates or understates the correct
value. For Abstain, the verifier provides the exact requirement it
could not establish, such as a missing formula or facts that cannot
be grounded in the filing. This provides the language model with a
concrete target for repair.

4
Experimental Setup
We evaluate VeriFin across six answer language models and three
datasets. Baseline comparisons cover four of these models. Within
each comparison, all methods evaluate the same questions and
fixed candidate answers, without regenerating the candidates. We
describe each method’s retrieval procedure and model configuration
below.
4.1
Datasets
XBRLFiling. We introduce XBRLFiling, a benchmark of 600 ques-
tions constructed from calculation relationships declared in corpo-
rate filings. Each question includes financial evidence, a reference
answer, and the corresponding calculation. We reduct the required
facts in the supplied evidence while retaining the evidence, requir-
ing the answer to be computed rather than copied. The benchmark
covers 15 financial metrics across income statements, balance sheets,
and cash-flow statements (see Table 1).
FinanceBench and TAT-DQA. We additionally evaluate on se-
lected numerical subsets of FinanceBench [8] and TAT-DQA [21],
containing 67 and 403 questions, respectively. TAT-DQA is a fi-
nancial document question-answering benchmark combining semi-
structured tables and unstructured text from financial reports. We
select questions requiring financial value extraction, numerical
comparisons, or calculations. We exclude questions requiring only
qualitative interpretation because our framework targets numerical
financial claims that remain challenging for LLMs [7]. For TAT-
DQA, we further require matching SEC filings with available XBRL
evidence and apply eligibility checks before running verification.
These selected subsets extend our evaluation beyond questions
constructed from XBRL calculation relationships.
4.2
Evaluated Models and Baselines
Financial Concepts and Formula Registry. Financial questions and
filings can use different terminology for the same concept, while
derived metrics require calculation definitions that may not be
stated in the filing. We curate a registry to connect financial terms
to filing concepts and provide documented formulas for verification.
This supplies a reference for checking the concepts and calculations
proposed by the verification planner.
The registry contains 59 metric entries, 17 arithmetic operation
rules, and 126 financial concepts. Entries specify formulas, required
inputs, and supporting references drawn from CFA Institute mate-
rials, US GAAP and IFRS taxonomies, and FinanceBench question
definitions. Nineteen metric entries are specific to FinanceBench.
The registry supplies definitions and mappings; numerical values
are retrieved from the evidence for each question.
Answer Models. To evaluate verification robustness across di-
verse model capabilities, candidate claims are generated using six
language models spanning different scales and architectures: fron-
tier closed models (GPT-5.5, Claude Haiku-4.5), open-weight
reasoning models (Qwen3-30B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B), and
domain-specialized financial models (Fin-o1-8B). Evaluating across
this models ensures our results reflect verifier performance rather
than specific generator biases. We served the openweight claim
generation models with vLLM, calling them through its OpenAI-
compatible endpoint; weights were downloaded from HuggingFace.
Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.5 were called through their respec-
tive APIs. The local models are accessed through OpenAPI com-
patible endpoints, and Claude Haiku 4.5 through Anthropic API.
generation settings vary across models and runs. For GPT-5.5 on
FinanceBench and TAT-DQA, we use low reasoning effort and an
8192-token output budget, without setting temperature.
Baselines. We compare VeriFin with an LLM Judge, FinDVer [19],
and VerifierFC [4]. All methods evaluate the same frozen candidate
claims, which are reused without regeneration. Evidence retrieval
and input construction follow each method’s respective evalua-
tion pipeline; no baseline receives gold answers, gold evidence, or
verdicts produced by another method.
• LLM judge: The model receives the question, retrieved evi-
dence, and fixed candidate claim, and predicts whether the
claim should be accepted as correct.
• FinDVer: We apply FinDVer’s released RAG-CoT method
using its text-embedding-3-large top-10 retrieval, original
prompts, and binary evaluation procedure. Following FinD-
Ver’s evaluation design across diverse LLM backbones, we
use Claude Haiku 4.5 as the verifier while preserving the
remaining components and settings of the released pipeline.
FinDVer produces a binary entailed/refuted verdict, which
we map to accept or reject. Because it returns one of these
two decisions for every evaluated claim, it has no abstentions
and therefore achieves 100% decision coverage.
• VerifierFC: We reproduce the GPT-4o-mini Best-of-10 con-
figuration reported for VerifierFC, using its full-filing BM25
retrieval and MiniLM reranking stages. We select this gener-
ator configuration because it achieves the highest reported
ClaimDecomp macro-F1 among the Best-of-10 configura-
tions evaluated in the original study. Following the reported
protocol, GPT-4o-mini generates ten reasoning-path–verdict
pairs for each claim. A Llama-3.2-3B LoRA path ranker,
trained on tuples comprising a claim, an LLM-generated rea-
soning path, and its predicted verdict, scores the candidates
and selects the highest-scoring verdict. We map supporting
and refuting verdicts to accept and reject, respectively, while
a conflicting verdict is treated as an abstention; VerifierFC
can therefore have less than 100% coverage. The authors did
not release the trained LoRA checkpoint, we reproduce the
ranker locally using their released training and development
data, implementation, and reported hyperparameters.

Inference and scoring. LLM Judge and FinDVer predict verifica-
tion verdicts from the candidate claim and evidence. VerifierFC uses
a trained verifier to score multiple generated reasoning paths and
returns the verdict associated with the highest-scoring path. Within
each comparison, we evaluate all methods against the same candi-
date correctness labels, using the scoring policy for each dataset.
These labels are used only for evaluation and are not supplied to
the verification methods. The numerical tolerance used by VeriFin
during verification is defined in §3.6.
Metrics. True accepts (TA) and false accepts (FA) count correct
and incorrect accepted claims, respectively. True rejects (TR) and
false rejects (FR) count incorrect and correct rejected claims. Abs
counts abstentions, including cases where candidate generation
fails.
We report false accepts, precision TA/(TA + FA), and decision
accuracy (TA + TR)/(TA + FA + TR + FR). Precision measures
correctness among accepted claims, while decision accuracy mea-
sures correctness among scored acceptance and rejection decisions.
Coverage is (𝑛−Abs)/𝑛, and the abstention rate is Abs/𝑛, where
𝑛includes every question. Cases with unresolved correctness are
excluded from precision and decision accuracy but remain in the
coverage calculation.
For repair, recovery requires an initially incorrect, rejected claim
to be revised into a correct answer that passes verification. Naive
repair uses one generic retry. Verifier-guided pass@1 measures
recovery on the first attempt, while pass@3 measures recovery
within three attempts. We report recovery rates together with re-
covered/scored counts for each condition.
4.3
Exploratory Repair Setup
As an auxiliary analysis, we examine whether diagnostics already
produced by VeriFin can support the revision of rejected numeri-
cal claims. When a claim is Violated, the verifier can use named
constraints from the solver’s unsatisfiable core to identify the filed
values, arithmetic relation, and claim bound involved in the incon-
sistency.
We consider two repair conditions:
• Naive Repair: the model receives the question, filing evi-
dence, previous answer, and a generic request to revise the
incorrect calculation.
• Verifier-Guided Repair: the model additionally receives
structured diagnostic feedback derived from the verification
failure.
The naive condition uses one retry. Verifier-guided pass@1 eval-
uates the first guided attempt, while pass@3 allows up to three
guided attempts and counts a case as recovered if any attempt suc-
ceeds. Every revised claim is checked using the same verification
procedure; it is counted as recovered only if it matches the gold
answer and receives a Verified verdict.
This exploratory analysis uses archived, condition-specific scored
subsets of true catches rather than a single matched set. We there-
fore report the recovery rates in Table 2 descriptively and do not
interpret differences between conditions as controlled effects.

5.2
Performance Across Claim Generating
Models
Table 4 reports VeriFin’s performance across six answer models
and three datasets. Across all 18 settings, none of the accepted
answers whose correctness could be assessed is incorrect. The pre-
cision is therefore 100% throughout, while the number of correct
answers accepted varies substantially across models and datasets.
The confidence intervals show how much evidence supports each
precision estimate. On FinanceBench, Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-
3.1-8B each have only ten correct accepted answers, giving a 95%
interval of [69.2%, 100%]. GPT-5.5 has 560 correct accepted answers
on XBRLFiling, with an interval of [99.3%, 100%]. Thus, our obser-
vation is that identical observed precision does not imply equally
precise estimates.
As shown in Figure 4 (left), VeriFin rejects every ground-truth-
incorrect candidate produced by the evaluated generators on XBRL-
Filing, yielding zero false accepts. Candidate claim accuracy nev-
ertheless varies widely, with cash-flow subtotals remaining partic-
ularly challenging (right).
5.3
Exploratory Verifier-Guided Repair
Table 2 presents an exploratory analysis of using solver-derived
diagnostics to guide repair attempts. On XBRLFiling, naive-repair
recovery is 6.5%, 26.1%, and 23.1% for Qwen3-30B, Fin-o1-8B, and
Claude Haiku 4.5, respectively. The corresponding verifier-guided
pass@1 rates are 46.3%, 57.0%, and 40.5%, while pass@3 reaches
47.7%, 69.9%, and 55.6%. On FinanceBench, generic retries recover
no cases in the scored subsets, whereas pass@3 reaches 50.0% for
Qwen3-30B, 9.1% for Fin-o1-8B, and 20.0% for Claude Haiku 4.5.
These results suggest that solver-derived feedback may help
models revise rejected answers and that additional guided attempts
may provide further recovery. However, because the conditions
were evaluated on separately scored archived subsets rather than
a single matched set, the results are descriptive rather than con-
trolled comparisons. Accordingly, this analysis provides an initial
illustration of the potential utility of verifier-guided repair.
6
Error Analysis
Abstentions. Our manual review identified three recurring causes
of abstention: failure to establish a supported formula, resolve the
required evidence, or obtain a usable numerical claim. In several
evidence-related cases, the necessary values were already present
in the source. The difficulty was connecting those values to the
financial concepts required by the question, showing that evidence
availability alone does not ensure successful verification.
False Rejects. Some false rejections arose from incorrect period
selection, misinterpreted financial quantities, and inconsistent sign
conventions. For example, a correct FY2025 answer was checked
against FY2026 facts, while another case treated net PP&E as gross
PP&E and applied an inappropriate depreciation adjustment. These
errors changed the financial problem encoded in the constraints: a
correct answer could then conflict with the constructed check. They
highlight the need to validate the interpretation of financial inputs
before symbolic checking. We distinguished these construction
errors from disagreements between the evaluation and verification
criteria.
Use of AI Assistants. Codex was used to assist with code imple-
mentation, LATEX formatting, and editorial revisions of the manu-
script. The authors independently conducted all research, designed
the experiments, performed the analyses, and formulated the scien-
tific claims and conclusions.
7
Conclusion
We introduced VeriFin, a neurosymbolic framework that verifies
numerical financial claims against facts and calculations established
independently of the candidate answer. Across six answer models
on XBRLFiling, FinanceBench, and TAT-DQA, the reported evalu-
ations yield zero observed false accepts among claims with known
correctness. Baseline comparisons show that higher precision can
coincide with accepting more correct answers, although the gains
vary across models and datasets. False rejections and abstentions
highlight the importance of correctly identifying financial concepts,
selecting evidence, and establishing the required calculation before
symbolic checking. These findings support neurosymbolic verifica-
tion for controlling false accepts in financial question answering.
Future work will focus on reducing abstentions through better ev-
idence resolution and financial interpretation while maintaining
high precision among accepted claims.
